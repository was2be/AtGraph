Getting Started

@Cam is a microscope camera app for the Mac. It shows the live image from a microscope camera, takes calibrated snapshots, movies and time-lapses, measures and counts what it sees, and saves files that Fiji, QuPath and @Graph read with their scale and notes.

Take Your First Image

  1. Connect the camera and open @Cam. The first time, macOS asks whether @Cam may use the camera: click Allow.
  2. With more than one camera attached, choose one from the camera menu at the left of the toolbar, or from the Camera menu.
  3. Live view starts by itself. Stop in the toolbar (⌘L) stops it, and Live starts it again.
  4. Set exposure and gain in the Camera tab, or leave them on Auto. A typed value takes effect when you press Return or leave the field.
  5. In View tab › Scale Bar, type the Objective and Camera adapter magnifications, so that measurements and the scale bar are in µm (see Scale Bar & Calibration).
  6. Click Snapshot in the toolbar (⌘I). The image is saved to the folder shown in Capture tab › Saving (Pictures › @Cam to begin with), or opens in a window to review first, as you choose there.

Start Without a Camera

With no camera open, @Cam shows the Welcome window. From it you can open a file (click Open File…, or drop one on the window), choose a camera, click Test Pattern to try everything without a camera, or open this guide. It lists recent files too. Help › Welcome to @Cam… (⌘@) shows it at any time; its checkbox, or @Cam › Settings… › General › Startup, stops it from appearing by itself.

The Window

  • The live image fills the window. Pinch, or use the zoom box in the toolbar. Hover over the image to read the pixel values at the bottom left.
  • The toolbar has Live, Snapshot, a handle for dragging the live image into another app, Record and the zoom.
  • The histogram under the image shows the brightness of the live image, and sets the display's black and white points.
  • The sidebar's tabs hold the controls. Sections a camera can't use are hidden: a webcam-style camera shows only the controls it reports, and has no Trigger & I/O tab.

Display settings (contrast, color tables, the histogram's levels) change only what you see and the "as displayed" captures. Camera-values TIFF and OME-TIFF snapshots always keep the camera's own values, so measurements stay true.

Cameras

@Cam works with webcam-style and machine-vision cameras, and with other cameras through a bridge.

Which Cameras Work

  • Webcam-style (UVC) cameras: most inexpensive microscope cameras (C-mount and eyepiece cameras from AmScope, OMAX, Swift, ToupTek's UVC models and others) work like a webcam. They give 8-bit color, or grayscale (Image tab › Format). Their controls appear when the camera reports them; a value under an automatic mode is grayed out until that mode is off.
  • Machine-vision cameras (USB3 Vision and GigE Vision, through the open-source Aravis library): full control of exposure, gain, black level, bit depth (8 or 16-bit), binning, region of interest, triggering and I/O lines. @Cam shows monochrome formats (Mono8 to Mono16, including the bit-packed Mono10p, Mono12p and Mono12Packed) and color: Bayer formats, packed or not, converted to 8-bit color (bilinear); RGB and BGR, with or without alpha and up to 16 bits a channel; and YUV and YCbCr (4:1:1, 4:2:2 and 4:4:4). Polarization cameras (a polarizer on each pixel, in groups of four) show the unpolarized image: each group is averaged into one pixel, at half the width and height, and the scale counts it as 2 × 2 binning. Older cameras that use GigE Vision 1.x names (ExposureTimeAbs, GainRaw and others) get the same controls; a setting a camera counts in its own steps is labeled (camera steps). The All Settings tab lists every feature the camera describes (Beginner, Expert or Guru).
  • Teledyne Lumenera INFINITY cameras with USB3 Vision firmware, such as the INFINITY8-2. See Switch an INFINITY Camera's Mode, below.
  • The Mac's own camera and an iPhone (Continuity Camera) work too, with the automatic settings macOS gives them.
  • Any other camera, through a bridge: a program that serves it to @Cam over the network (see Bridges: Other Cameras).
  • Test Pattern, built into @Cam, for trying everything without a camera. Its Exposure time sets the brightness (10 ms is the pattern as designed). Choose it from the camera menu; @Cam never opens it by itself.

With several cameras attached, @Cam opens the one you last chose, otherwise a machine-vision camera, then a bridge or an external webcam-style camera, then the built-in one. Choose another from the camera menu in the toolbar or from the Camera menu.

Show Two Cameras

  1. With a second camera attached, choose it in Camera tab › Two Cameras › Second camera.
  2. Under Show, choose Side by side, One above the other, Picture in picture (with Inset size and Corner) or Merged.
  3. For Merged, drag the slider between Camera 1 and Camera 2, and under Combine choose Cross-fade or Add (overlay), for fluorescence over brightfield, say. Under Alignment, set Shift right, Shift down, Size and Rotate to line the two images up; Reset Alignment starts again. The alignment is remembered for the pair.
  4. Set camera 2's color table, contrast, exposure and gain in the same section. For its other settings (white balance, region, format), click Swap Cameras to make it the main camera, set them, then swap back.
  • The other tabs adjust the main camera, which sits at the top left at full size. Measurements and the scale bar refer to it.
  • Snapshots and movies record the combined image as displayed. With a camera-values format chosen, each camera's own image is saved as well (_camera1, _camera2), and the description records how old camera 2's frame was (Camera2Age=).
  • A snapshot opened in a window to review offers only the as-displayed formats for the combined image. Its Open in @Graph sends each camera's values.

Trigger a Machine-Vision Camera

  1. In Trigger & I/O tab › Trigger, set Trigger mode to On and Source to Software.
  2. Click Software Trigger. The camera takes exactly one exposure each time; no cable is needed.

Hardware triggers and strobe outputs use the camera's I/O port; the I/O Lines section lists each line as the camera reports it. To save one image per trigger pulse, run a time-lapse set to Every frame (Tools tab).

Drive a Light From the Camera

A camera with output lines can switch an LED or flash with each exposure, so the sample is lit only while the camera exposes (less bleaching and heat). Wire the light's trigger input to one of the camera's output pins (see its manual), then:

  1. In Trigger & I/O tab › Light Output, choose the Output line it is wired to.
  2. Set Light to On during exposure, or to Pulse for a flash of set length: type the Delay after exposure starts and the Pulse length, in ms. Pulse appears when the camera has timers.
  3. Check it with Trigger mode On and Software Trigger: the light should flash once per click. Off leaves the line low.

Light Output uses the parts all standard cameras share (an output line, its source, and a timer). Cameras with their own strobe controls (Lumenera, Allied Vision, The Imaging Source) also show them in the Strobe section; use one or the other.

Switch an INFINITY Camera's Mode

An INFINITY camera's firmware has two modes: USB3 Vision, used by @Cam, and Infinity, used by Teledyne's INFINITY ANALYZE. A camera in Infinity mode can't be opened until you switch it.

  1. Quit INFINITY ANALYZE, and attach only one camera in Infinity mode: with two attached, the switch is refused.
  2. Choose Camera › Switch to USB3 Vision Mode…, or click the same button on the live window or in the Welcome window.
  3. Click Switch. @Cam checks the camera, keeps a backup, and changes one setting in the camera's memory.
  4. Unplug the camera and plug it back in.

Camera › Switch to Infinity Mode… switches it back for INFINITY ANALYZE.

Don't unplug the camera while @Cam switches its mode.

USB Devices

Camera › USB Devices… lists the camera-like USB devices attached to the Mac and the mode each is in. @Cam reads only the standard description every USB device gives of itself; it writes nothing to any device. Show all USB devices adds hubs, keyboards, disks and the like. Bridges: Other Cameras

A bridge is a separate program that drives a camera @Cam has no driver for and serves its controls and frames to @Cam over the network. @Cam treats a bridged camera like any other, so live view, calibration, corrections, snapshots, time-lapse, movies, mosaics and two-camera views all work with it. A bridge can run on this Mac or on another computer, in any language, using whatever software the camera's maker provides for it.

Ready-Made Bridges

atcam-bridges.zip on the @Cam website has bridges for ToupTek cameras (including cameras ToupTek makes for other brands) and XIMEA cameras, which run natively on Macs with Apple silicon. Each gives @Cam the sensor's raw values, monochrome or color at 8 bits or the sensor's full depth, or the camera's own color, with exposure, gain, resolution, binning, region of interest, trigger and, on cooled cameras, the cooler.

Use a Bridge

  1. For a ready-made bridge, install the camera maker's software first: ToupTek's SDK, or XIMEA's macOS package.
  2. Start the bridge in Terminal. Its README says how, step by step.
  3. If the bridge doesn't announce itself on the network, type its address under @Cam › Settings… › Cameras › Camera Bridges, as host:port (for example localhost:48620 for the reference bridge on this Mac), and click Add.
  4. Choose the bridge from the camera menu, where it appears under its own name next to the cameras plugged into the Mac. The first time, macOS asks whether @Cam may look for devices on the local network.
  • The Camera, Image and Trigger & I/O tabs show the bridge's controls under the usual labels when the bridge uses the standard names (exposure time, gain, automatic modes, trigger mode, software trigger and so on). Anything else is in the All Settings tab. The Info tab names the bridge.
  • A bridge that stops answering is reported as disconnected and left out of the camera menu until you pick it again or add it again. In Settings, a bridge added by address is marked "not answering", with a Retry button.

Write a Bridge

The protocol is small: one TCP connection, with messages that are a line of JSON (and, for frames, the pixels after it). The full description (PROTOCOL.md) and a reference bridge in Python with a simulated camera (atcam_bridge.py) are in atcam-bridges.zip on the @Cam website.

  • Run the reference bridge with python3 atcam_bridge.py: a simulated camera with exposure, gain, frame rate, a trigger and several pixel formats, for trying @Cam without hardware or testing a setup.
  • To bridge a real camera, subclass its Camera class: list the controls, and return frames from the maker's software.
  • Frames can be 8- or 16-bit monochrome, Bayer (@Cam converts them to color, and can save the raw mosaic) or RGB.

Bridges are also how Micro-Manager's camera adapters can reach @Cam: a bridge on pymmcore, Micro-Manager's Python interface, can serve whatever camera Micro-Manager drives on that computer.

Live View & Display

Apart from the Exposure and Gain sliders, the settings on this page change only how the image is shown and the "as displayed" captures, never the camera's values.

Zoom

⌘0 fits the image to the window, ⌘1 shows it at 100% (one camera pixel per screen pixel), and ⌘= and ⌘- zoom in and out; these act on the window in front, an opened image included. You can also pinch on the trackpad, or type a percentage in the toolbar's zoom box and press Return.

Set the Contrast on the Histogram

  1. On the histogram, drag the blue line to set the black point and the orange line to set the white point.
  2. Drag the yellow handle between them to bend the curve (brighter or darker mid-tones).
  3. Double-click the histogram, or click ↺, to reset.

View tab › Display has Contrast (Full range, the default, Auto contrast or Manual), Display gamma, Logarithmic histogram (it brings out a faint signal on a dark background) and, for color cameras, Separate red, green and blue histograms.

The histogram's top line gives the image's lowest, highest and mean values. When the values come in steps, as at high gain or with 12-bit values in a 16-bit format, it also gives the number of levels and the step between them. Fewer levels mean coarser intensities: a lower gain with a longer exposure gives more.

Under the histogram, Exposure and Gain sliders set the camera's exposure time (in ms) and gain without opening the Camera tab. They are dimmed when the camera won't take a value (under Auto, on many cameras).

Color Tables

View tab › Display › Color table shows a grayscale image in false color: Inverted, Green, Red, Blue, Cyan, Magenta, Yellow, Fire, Viridis, or Emission wavelength (λ), with a wavelength slider and common fluorophores. Highlight saturated pixels marks them in red, or in cyan when the color table is itself reddish. A camera whose 16-bit format carries fewer bits is marked saturated at its own top value.

Smooth Live View

View tab › Display › Smooth live view averages the last 2, 4, 8 or 16 frames (Off by default), for a calmer live image of dim fluorescence. Moving things leave a short trail. Measurements and camera-values snapshots use the unsmoothed image; for less noise in saved images, average frames (see Snapshots & Movies › Average Frames).

Focus Aid

  1. In Camera tab › Focus Aid, turn on Focus meter, and under Measure choose Center of the image (the default) or Whole image.
  2. Turn the fine focus until the meter, at the top right of the image, peaks: 100% is the sharpest image seen recently. The meter's reset button forgets the best so far.
  3. To mark the sharpest edges in green, turn on Highlight sharp edges (focus peaking).

The meter doesn't depend on brightness or contrast, and a best from a while ago fades over about a quarter of a minute. Both aids are on screen only.

Eyepiece Overlay

  1. In View tab › Eyepiece Overlay, choose Grid, Reticle (a crosshair with divisions) or Circles.
  2. Type the spacing in µm and choose a Color.
  3. To have it in captures, turn on Include in snapshots and movies.

The spacing is in real µm on the specimen, from the scale bar's calibration, like an eyepiece graticule. View tab › Display also has a simple Crosshair.

Present the Live Image

  1. Choose View › Present Live Image on …, which names each display, or View tab › Display › Present Full Screen. ⌥⌘P presents on the first display. The live image is shown alone, full screen, with the scale bar and measurements.
  2. To end, press Esc or double-click on that display, press ⌥⌘P again, or choose View › Stop Presenting.

Banners Over the Image

A banner over the image shows progress while something runs (a mosaic, a focus stack, a time-lapse, averaging). Drag it by its grip (≡) to move it out of the way; double-click the grip to put it back.

Scale Bar & Calibration

The scale turns pixels into µm for the scale bar, measurements, the eyepiece overlay and the scale saved in every image.

Set the Scale

  1. In View tab › Scale Bar, type the Objective magnification (10× by default) or pick a common one. For several lenses in a row type their combined magnification, and use 1× for a camera without a microscope objective.
  2. Type the Camera adapter magnification (1× by default): that of the C-mount adapter between microscope and camera, often printed on it (0.5×, for example).
  3. Type the Sensor pixel size from the camera's data sheet, or click Calibrate… to measure it. For an INFINITY8-2, click Use 9 µm (INFINITY8-2). It is remembered for each camera, and for a webcam-style camera for each resolution.

Calibrate With a Stage Micrometer

  1. Put a stage micrometer (a slide with a ruled scale) under the microscope and focus on it.
  2. Set Objective and Camera adapter in View tab › Scale Bar, then click Calibrate….
  3. Drag along a known distance on the micrometer, as long as you can.
  4. Type its length in µm and click Set Scale.

@Cam works out the sensor pixel size from the line, so the scale stays right when you change the objective setting. Check the calibration once against a stage micrometer even when you know the pixel size.

A Camera Without a Pixel Size

A camera @Cam hasn't had a pixel size for (a new camera, or any camera after @Cam › Settings… › Cameras › Reset for All Cameras…) never takes another camera's. Its Sensor pixel size field says "not set", no scale bar is drawn, measurements are in pixels, and its images are saved without a scale, with "Scale=not calibrated" in their description.

A Camera With an Ordinary Lens

A camera with its own lens (a CCTV lens, a C- or CS-mount lens, a webcam) has no fixed scale: how much of the subject a pixel covers depends on how far away the subject is, and a wide-angle lens also stretches the edges of the picture. For such a camera:

  1. In View tab › Scale Bar, set Optics to Camera lens.
  2. @Cam then draws no scale bar, measures in pixels and saves images without a scale, with "Scale=none (camera lens)" in their description. The setting is remembered for that camera, and the pixel size is kept: set Optics back to Microscope when the camera goes on a microscope. A Mac's built-in camera and an iPhone (Continuity Camera) start on Camera lens.

The Scale Bar

ControlWhat it does
Show scale bar (⌘B)Draws the bar on the live image (off by default).
Bar lengthEmpty (the default) picks a round length automatically. A length longer than the image is replaced by the longest round length that fits; the label always gives the length drawn.
PositionBottom right (the default), Bottom left, Top right or Top left.

While it is shown, the bar is burned into PNG, JPEG and as-displayed TIFF snapshots and into movies. Once the camera's pixel size is set, every image @Cam saves also stores the scale itself, bar or not; Files, Metadata & Other Apps lists where each format keeps it.

How the Scale Is Computed

µm per image pixel = sensor pixel size × binning × decimation ÷ (objective × adapter), for each axis.

  • When binning or decimation differs between the axes, the pixels aren't square: lengths and areas are then in pixels, angles are measured on the specimen, and saved images record the scale of each axis. An opened image with a scale for each axis is measured the same way, and Save As… keeps both scales.
  • A webcam-style camera at a resolution not calibrated yet starts from another resolution's calibration, scaled by the widths, and says so. Calibrate at each resolution you measure with.

Image Corrections

Corrections are applied to every frame before anything else, so they are in the live view, measurements, every snapshot (camera-values TIFFs too), movies, focus stacks and mosaics, and are noted in each image's description. Each is remembered for each camera.

Correct Shading With a Flat Field

Shading correction evens out uneven lighting and dark corners, and for color cameras makes the empty field neutral.

  1. Move to an empty, clean part of the slide (or take the slide out), with the light and objective as for your samples.
  2. Click Image tab › Shading Correction › Take Flat Field. @Cam averages 16 frames.
  3. Optionally, with the light off, click Take Dark Frame to remove the camera's own background too. It can come before or after the flat field.

Correct shading turns the correction off and on; Clear removes it. Take the flat field again after changing the objective, the light or the resolution: a correction taken at another resolution isn't applied, and the section says so.

Set the White Balance From the Image

  1. With a color camera, click Camera tab › White Balance › Set from Image….
  2. Click a plain white or gray part of the image (an empty part of the slide) that is neither saturated nor very dark.

Rotate and Flip

Image tab › Orientation has Flip horizontally, Flip vertically and Rotate (90° right, 180° or 90° left), for eyepiece and C-mount cameras that show the image turned or mirrored. A machine-vision camera that can flip the image itself does the flips in the camera.

In-Camera Processing

Image tab › In-Camera Processing holds what the camera itself does before it sends the image: Gamma, Contrast, Brightness and Lookup table (a curve stored in the camera with the camera maker's software) on machine-vision cameras, and Brightness, Contrast, Gamma, Saturation, Hue and Sharpness on webcam-style cameras. The Lookup table switch is hidden while the camera's table is empty.

These settings change the values the camera sends, and camera-values images (TIFF, OME-TIFF, @Graph image) keep them as changed; the histogram and View tab › Display change only how @Cam shows the image. For intensity measurements leave the camera's Gamma at 1 and its Lookup table off: @Cam warns, in this section and in the Info tab, when a machine-vision camera's are not. Each saved image's description records these settings (InCameraGamma, InCameraContrast, InCameraBrightness, InCameraLUT).

Snapshots & Movies

A snapshot saves one image and a recording saves a movie, with the notes, folder, names and formats set in the Capture tab.

Take a Snapshot

  1. In Capture tab › Saving, click Choose… to pick the one folder everything captured goes to.
  2. Under Snapshots & movies, choose Save automatically to the folder (the default) or Open in a new window (save manually).
  3. In Capture tab › Snapshot, type the Image name and choose the Format (see Formats, below).
  4. Click Snapshot in the toolbar (⌘I).
  5. If the snapshot opened in a window, choose the format, scale bar and measurements there and click Save…. Closing the window asks before an unsaved capture is discarded.

Session Notes

Capture tab › Session Notes has Specimen, Stain or label, Operator and Note. They stay until you change them, and are saved in every image (its description, and the caption, keywords and author that Finder, Spotlight, Preview and Photos show) and in movies. Turn on Add specimen and stain to file names for names like Image_Liver_H-E_001.tif.

File Names

  • Capture tab › Saving › File names: Name and number (the default) gives Image_001, Image_002…, with separate counts for snapshots and movies; Name, date and time gives Image_20261018_153000.
  • With numbers, Saving shows the next names. Reset to 001 starts again, and a new name starts its own count. Numbers already used in the folder are skipped, so no file is replaced.
  • Time-lapse images always save to the folder, named with the date and time.
  • Show in Finder, or Camera › Show Captures in Finder, shows the last file saved.

Formats

Each format says what it keeps: the camera values (what the sensor measured, for measuring) or the image as displayed (with your contrast settings, for showing), and whether it is lossless or compressed. Measure brightness only in a camera-values format.

FormatWhat it keeps
TIFF – camera values, losslessThe camera's values (8 or 16-bit) with the scale and the bit depth, for measuring in Fiji or @Graph. Nothing is drawn on it. A grayscale image also records the display's black and white points as ImageJ does, so Fiji and @Graph open it looking as it did on screen; the values are unchanged.
OME-TIFF – camera values, losslessThe camera's values with an OME-XML header: exact pixel size in µm, bit depth, notes, objective, the time it was taken, and the measurements and annotations as regions. Read directly by Fiji (Bio-Formats), QuPath, Python and @Graph.
@Graph image – camera values, losslessAn @Graph document: opens in @Graph ready to measure, with the pixel size, bit depth and date. Measurements and annotations are not included (use OME-TIFF for those). Opens only in @Graph, so keep an OME-TIFF too when others need the data.
TIFF – as displayed, losslessWhat you see: contrast, color table, scale bar, measurements and overlays burned in (8-bit).
PNG – as displayed, lossless / JPEG – as displayed, compressedThe same, for slides, documents and the web.
  • Camera values are saved exactly as the camera gave them and never rescaled: a 12-bit camera's values stay 0 to 4,095 in a 16-bit file, and each file records the bit depth. @Cam, @Graph and Fiji stretch the display when they open it; Preview and Finder thumbnails don't, so they show such images dark.
  • Bayer color cameras and webcam-style cameras deliver color that @Cam converts to 8 bits per color (Bayer: bilinear; webcams: as macOS decodes them). Their camera-values formats keep that converted color, and the Format menu says so.

Raw Bayer Mosaic

A Bayer camera's raw mosaic is the sensor's own values at its full bit depth: one gray channel in which each pixel saw red, green or blue only, so it looks finely checkered. Keep it to make the color image in @Graph (Correct › Debayer), in more exact arithmetic than the 8-bit view.

  1. In Capture tab › Saving › Bayer camera saves, choose Color (the 8-bit color, the default), Raw Bayer Mosaic, or Both.
  2. Choose a camera-values format and take the snapshot. Both saves the color file and the mosaic beside it, named with _raw. The as-displayed formats are always color.
  • The Bayer pattern (RGGB, BGGR, GRBG or GBRG, the colors of the top-left 2 × 2 pixels) is recorded in the standard TIFF/EP tags (CFARepeatPatternDim and CFAPattern) of every TIFF, OME-TIFF and stack page, and as BayerPattern=RGGB RawBayer=12bit in the description. @Graph reads it.
  • The mosaic is never shading-corrected or white-balanced, so it stays the sensor's measurement. Correct it in @Graph (Correct › Shading, with a raw dark and flat) before debayering.
  • An OME-TIFF stack recording and a time-lapse stack hold the mosaics when the setting is Raw Bayer Mosaic or Both. A mosaic and a focus stack are saved in color whatever the setting.
  • @Cam's own windows show a saved mosaic in color. Turn off Show the … mosaic in color, in an opened image's sidebar, to see it as recorded; the pixel readout then gives the recorded values. Measurements and saved copies always use the values as stored.

Average Frames

  1. In Capture tab › Snapshot, type the number of frames beside Average (2 to 256), or step it with the arrows. @Cam says below how long a snapshot will take.
  2. Keep the specimen still and take the snapshot. A banner on the image shows the progress; Cancel (or Esc) stops it.
  3. Reset to 1 goes back to single frames.

Averaging reduces noise, for dim fluorescence for example. The average is rounded to the camera's own levels, so the image stays in camera values and compares with single shots: it lowers the noise but adds no levels (an 8-bit camera's average is still 8-bit). The setting is kept for every camera, and the description records the number of frames.

Record a Movie

  1. In Capture tab › Movie, type the Movie name and choose the Format.
  2. Click Record in the toolbar (⌘R).
  3. Click Stop Recording (⌘R again). The movie is saved to the folder, or opens in its own window to review and save, as Capture tab › Saving sets.

Time-lapse, Focus Stacks & Merging

The Tools tab takes a time-lapse, a focus stack through a thick specimen, and channels merged into one color image.

Take a Time-lapse

  1. Set the Format and Average in Capture tab › Snapshot: a time-lapse uses both.
  2. In Tools tab › Time-lapse, under Shots choose At an interval and type the interval, in seconds or milliseconds (10 seconds by default), or choose Every frame for one image for every frame the camera delivers.
  3. Type the number of Images (10 by default; 0 = until stopped).
  4. Optionally turn on Also make a movie of the images, with its Movie speed, and Also make one TIFF stack of all images.
  5. Click Start Time-lapse (⌘T). A banner over the live image shows how many images are taken and to go, with a Stop button; ⌘T stops it too.
  • The movie and the TIFF stack are made when the time-lapse ends, in the same folder as the images.
  • The TIFF stack is one multi-page OME-TIFF that Fiji, QuPath and @Graph open as a time series with each image's time and the scale (ImageJ opens it as a stack). With it on, the images aren't kept as files of their own unless Also keep each image as its own file is on. Its switch is dimmed while the snapshot format is @Graph image.
  • An interval shorter than the camera's frame time, or Every frame, gives one image per frame, as fast as the camera and the disk allow. A frame is never saved twice, and frames @Cam can't keep up with are skipped and counted.
  • With a hardware trigger (Trigger & I/O tab › Trigger mode On, a line as the Source), Every frame saves one image per pulse. For every frame in one file, record an OME-TIFF stack instead.

Time-lapse Timing

  • Each image's description records its number and its time from the start, which includes any time the Mac spent asleep. While a time-lapse or a recording runs, @Cam keeps the Mac from sleeping when idle; closing the lid or choosing Sleep still puts it to sleep.
  • An interval that passes without an image (the Mac was asleep or busy, there was no new frame, or the last shot was still averaging) is counted as missed. The count shows in the Tools tab and when the time-lapse ends.
  • If an image can't be saved (a full disk, say), the time-lapse stops and @Cam says why. An OME-TIFF stack recording stops the same way, keeping the frames recorded before it with their times.

Take a Focus Stack

A focus stack (extended depth of field) is for specimens thicker than the depth of focus.

  1. Choose Camera › Start Focus Stack (⇧⌘F), or click Start Focus Stack in the Tools tab.
  2. Turn the fine focus slowly through the whole depth while @Cam collects frames. Keep the stage still.
  3. Click Finish on the banner, or press ⇧⌘F again. The result is saved like a snapshot and opens in a window.

Each part of the result comes mostly from the frames where it was sharpest, blended so there are no seams. Sharpness is judged above each frame's noise, so noise doesn't pass for detail. Where no frame is sharper than the others (the background, or a part that stays in focus), the frames are averaged, which also lowers the noise.

A shutter sound plays when a time-lapse or focus stack starts, and a chime when it ends (turn them off in @Cam › Settings… › General › Sounds).

Merge Channels

Merge Channels combines channels into one color image, for fluorescence channels taken one after another, or fluorescence over brightfield.

  1. With the first channel in the live view, click Add Current Image as Channel in Tools tab › Merge Channels. The Merge Channels window opens.
  2. Change the filter and add the next channel the same way.
  3. To add images taken earlier (TIFF, PNG, JPEG and the other formats @Cam opens), drop them on the Merge Channels window or click its Add Images…. Each is named after its file and keeps its scale, and a color image counts by its brightness. A raw Bayer mosaic is first debayered to 8-bit color, as the live view shows it.
  4. Give each channel a color (or an emission wavelength) and set its levels.
  5. Click Save Merged Image… to save a PNG or TIFF, with the scale bar if it is on.

All channels must have the same resolution. The file records the scale the channels were taken at, bar or not; channels taken at different scales (another binning, say), or a channel without a scale, leave it uncalibrated, and the window says so.

Mosaics (Stitching)

A mosaic joins many fields into one large image, for an area bigger than the field of view. It works with an ordinary manual stage, or a microscope that moves over a fixed stage: @Cam follows the live image as the field moves, in any direction.

Take a Mosaic

  1. Use manual exposure, and if you can take a flat field first (see Image Corrections), so the tiles match.
  2. Put one corner of the area in view and choose Camera › Start Mosaic (⇧⌘M), or click Start Mosaic in the Tools tab. This field is the first tile.
  3. Move the stage (or the microscope). A map at the top right shows the tiles taken so far and the field's outline: green where a new tile would overlap well (about a third is ideal), orange where it overlaps too much, red where it overlaps too little or the field is lost.
  4. When the outline is green, click Add Tile (or press ⇧⌘M). Go along a row, then down a little and back, overlapping each new field with the tiles beside and above it.
  5. Click Finish. @Cam aligns the overlapping tiles to the pixel, evens out brightness differences and blends the seams. The mosaic is saved like a snapshot, with the scale. While it stitches, Cancel stops it and keeps the tiles, to add more or click Finish again.
  • Add tiles automatically when the field stops moving (Tools tab) adds a tile whenever the field rests for a moment on new ground that still overlaps.
  • Undo removes the last tile; Cancel ends the mosaic without saving.
  • If the outline turns red and the banner says Lost, move back over a part already taken: @Cam finds the field again.
  • A mosaic holds up to 400 tiles, each a full camera frame. Use binning or a lower resolution for large areas.

Overlaps need detail to line up: a field of empty glass or plain background can't be placed. Move on to where there is some structure, and the mosaic carries on.

Brightness Across a Mosaic

The mosaic keeps the camera's values (8 or 16-bit, or color), times each tile's gain. Evening out brightness gives each tile a gain between 0.5 and 2, so that the overlaps match; values pushed above the camera's full scale are clipped. The gains are written in the file's description, in the order the tiles were taken (MosaicGains=1.000,0.930,…). For intensity measurements across the mosaic, use manual exposure and a flat field so the gains stay near 1, and check them in the description.

Stitch Image Files

Stitch Images… makes a mosaic from image files: fields taken earlier, with another camera, or by another program. No camera is needed.

  1. Choose File › Stitch Images…, or click Stitch Images… in the Tools tab.
  2. Drop the images on the Stitch Images window, or click Add Images…. Any format @Cam opens will do, in any order, as long as all are the same size and kind (all grayscale or all color, all 8 or all 16 bits). Raw Bayer mosaics are first debayered to 8-bit color, as the live view shows them, and count as color.
  3. Under Save as, choose TIFF or OME-TIFF to keep the values as read, or PNG or JPEG for a picture (16-bit values stretched to their range).
  4. Click Stitch…. @Cam finds which images overlap and where, then aligns and blends them as for a live mosaic. Cancel stops it; nothing is saved.
  5. Choose where to save the result, which then opens.
  • Neighboring images should overlap by about a third, with some detail in the overlap. An image that overlaps none of the others is left out, and the window names it.
  • Stitching more than 40 images is quickest when the files are named in the order they were taken.
  • The mosaic keeps the scale when every image has the same one.
  • The stitching is planar: images are shifted, not rotated or warped, which suits microscope fields. Photographs taken by turning a camera need a panorama program.
  • Up to 400 images make one mosaic. The window says when the mosaic is likely to be larger than @Cam can make.

Opening Saved Images

Open saved images to adjust levels and color tables, and to measure, count and annotate them as on the live image. The original file is never changed.

Open an Image

  1. Choose File › Open File… (⌘O), drop the file on any @Cam window or on its Dock icon, or in Finder choose Open With › @Cam. File › Open Recent lists the files opened, saved and exported most recently.
  2. If the image has no scale, turn on Calibrated under Scale in the window's sidebar and type the pixel size.
  3. Adjust the display, measure and annotate, then keep a copy with Export… or Save As… (see Save a Copy, below).

What Opens

  • Images: TIFF (also OME-TIFF), PNG, JPEG, HEIC and HEIF (iPhone photos), WebP, BMP, GIF, JPEG 2000 and DNG (as macOS develops it). 16-bit images keep their full values; 16-bit color images are rounded to 8 bits per color.

  • A stack, such as an OME-TIFF recording from @Cam, opens with a Frame slider in the sidebar's Image section. The frame's time from the start of the recording is shown when the file has it. The display settings, scale, measurements and annotations stay as they are from frame to frame.

  • A file @Cam can't open is named in a note.
